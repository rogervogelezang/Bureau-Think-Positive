---
title: "Four helpful thoughts for dealing with aggression"
description: "Four thoughts that help you avoid taking aggression personally and keep sight of the underlying care need behind the behaviour."
category: "Aggression & de-escalation"
sourceSlug: "vier-helpende-gedachtes"
---

1. Remember that aggression is often a sign of powerlessness, fear or a lack of self-confidence. It's often nothing personal, even when the care recipient makes arguments that feel like a personal attack. A care recipient using instrumental aggression is simply trying to get their way by playing on your feelings. They're not really focused on you — they're trying to reach their goal by making you feel bad. Once you see through this mechanism, you'll notice you're less easily thrown off balance. The insults no longer get to you, which lets you stay calm and keep thinking clearly.

2. Remember that if the care recipient reacts with anger or aggression, that's their problem. They always have the choice to raise their complaint differently, more politely. So don't let yourself get provoked, because it's not your problem but theirs — you don't owe anything and you don't have to do anything. Keep thinking consciously, make your own choices, and don't let yourself be pressured.

3. See the aggressive care recipient as a care recipient. Behind aggression there's often a request for help and an underlying care need. Look past the aggression and search for that care need, whatever the inappropriate way in which it's being expressed.

4. See the care recipient as someone unwell and in need, who simply — through pain or grief — no longer has the energy to express their care need in an appropriate way. Or see them as someone who never had the chance, for example because of a lack of good role models, to learn how to express their wishes appropriately.
