Maslow's pyramid is a hierarchical ordering of needs published by Abraham Maslow in 1943. Maslow arranged what he considered the universal needs of human beings into this hierarchy. According to his theory, a person only strives to satisfy the needs placed higher in the hierarchy once the needs placed lower down have been satisfied.

## The higher and lower needs

Maslow's hierarchy of needs looks like this:

- **Organic or physiological needs** — these physiological needs relate to the organism's homeostasis and physical balance. They include the need for sleep, food, drink and the elimination of waste. Maslow also classifies sex and other bodily matters such as exercise and comfort under this heading.
- **Need for safety and security** — the individual seeks protection within an organised group, whether small or large — the neighbourhood, the family or the company, for example. Typical examples are housing, work and relationships.
- **Need for belonging** — the need for friendship, love and positive social relationships.
- **Need for appreciation, recognition and self-respect** — needs that increase one's competence and standing within a group; attaching importance to social status.
- **Need for self-realisation or self-actualisation** — the need to develop and give value to one's own personality and mental growth potential. The social environment cannot be discounted as a supporting basis for this drive towards actualisation.
- **Need for self-transcendence** — in the later stages of his life, Maslow refined his view of self-actualisation and placed the emphasis on self-transcendence.

Maslow also called the physiological need the 'lower fundamental need'. The four higher needs are the 'higher fundamental needs'. The key word here is 'fundamental': according to Maslow, failing to satisfy these fundamental needs diminishes a person's full humanity and blocks human potential. The need for growth, in Maslow's view, is not fundamental — a person can only realise themselves (grow) once the fundamental needs have been satisfied at a minimum level.

In later research, Maslow concluded that every person is caught between the fundamental need for security and the need for growth towards new experience. To be able to grow, a person must reduce the appeal of the 'safe situation' and increase the perceived risk of 'growth'. The moment a person settles into their habits yet still wants to gain new experiences, they break through this safe situation.

## How Maslow's pyramid works in practice

Maslow argued that every living being pursues the same needs. Once a need is satisfied, the individual moves up to the next level. If a step is missing or falls away, the individual must satisfy that need again before being able to climb further — it is not possible to skip particular levels.

**Example:** someone walking through a museum (in search of beauty) who becomes hungry (a physiological need) will first try to eat something before continuing their search for more beauty. After eating, the person moves back up to the sixth level, because all the levels in between are still being met. If, however, they receive news that their house has burned down, they will first return to that need in order to 'satisfy' it. All the intermediate levels can be passed through at different moments and are not automatically skipped.

Once the basic needs have been satisfied, other needs come into play: the social ones.
