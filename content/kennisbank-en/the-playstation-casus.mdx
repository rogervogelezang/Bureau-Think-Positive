title: "The PlayStation Case: the power of a positive approach"
description: "A practice story from youth care about how focusing on what's going well, rather than moralising, creates room for growth and trust."
category: "Personal"

I was working at a youth care institution somewhere in the country.

I was on a one-to-one shift with a young man who'd gone off the rails, and he had a visit from a fellow group member. They started playing Grand Theft Auto — a video game where you can steal cars and rob people like a real-life gangster.

The boys were enjoying the game and all the ways it let them do things that aren't allowed in real life.

Instead of taking a moralistic approach and lecturing the boys about norms and values — which is what you might expect from a care worker — I used the situation to focus on the positives I could see.

I saw, for instance, that the two of them worked well together as a team during the game. I saw that the boys were looking for recognition for the tricks they pulled off steering the cars through dangerous situations in the game.

I saw that they were genuinely happy for each other whenever they made it to the next level — complimenting one another.

There was a lot going right, and I realise that we often only stop to look at what a boy like this is doing wrong. We tend to focus on the imperfections and forget to look at what is actually going well.

When I genuinely complimented the boys on what I saw, I noticed they found it hard to accept those compliments. I realised this was because they aren't used to being approached from a positive angle.

They simply didn't know what to do with the positive attention. This can be a pitfall, but because I've often seen a boy like this not know how to handle it, I knew what to expect.

After the praise and compliments, when an awkward silence took hold of the situation, I managed to break it by telling the boys that when someone gives you a compliment, you can simply respond with a plain 'thank you'. After I told them this, the boys themselves gave me a 'thank you' — for the help I'd offered.

That was a lovely moment — a valuable learning moment for each of us.

What do I take from this? Simply this: we can choose to emphasise everything that's negative, but what does that actually achieve? What if, instead, you pick up on the small positive things you see — and what if picking up on the positive is the start of growth and trust?
