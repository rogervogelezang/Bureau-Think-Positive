A 'hands-on coach' combines the roles of support worker, coach and social pedagogue, and is brought in during emergency situations. The hands-on coach first takes on the role of support worker, to experience directly on the floor where the heart of the situation lies. From that position, he helps the team build a clear picture of the situation and of the care recipient.

From this starting point, he then gets to work with the necessary interventions, based on Design Thinking. When the situation is urgent, he first focuses on safety by putting structures in place and setting clear boundaries. Usually, this is enough to give the team and the care recipient an initial sense of basic safety. From there, his role shifts, and he works in an increasingly coaching capacity to help the team move forward.

At a later stage, the hands-on coach sits down with the personal support worker and social pedagogue to make the findings transferable to the rest of the team. The difference with the conventional way of working with care recipients whose situation has stalled lies in the persuasive approach: the situation is approached from different angles (support worker, coach, social pedagogue). The team and its social pedagogue always retain ownership and responsibility for the strategy to be followed — the whole concept is based on empowering both the team and the care recipient.

## Where this approach comes from

This approach emerged after 15 years of working as a specialist support worker with care recipients whose situations had stalled, often people with a mild intellectual disability and a disharmonious developmental profile. The care recipient was regularly misjudged: attachment issues became an obstacle for the team in setting clear boundaries, resulting in unclear parent-child roles in which the care recipient became overloaded with too much freedom, control and responsibility. Institutional standards sometimes got in the way too.

Maybe things aren't going well at home, and you've already tried everything, but you can't break the negative spiral. Or maybe you've ended up in a crisis and no longer know which way to turn. Sometimes an outsider, whose view isn't clouded by everything that's happened, can help by reflecting the situation back to you from a helicopter view — and suddenly you know again: that's where I need to go.

You simply can't expect a team to find its own way out of a crisis. Often that doesn't work, and shame about not being able to solve the situation themselves gets in the way of bringing in outside help — while it's precisely an outsider who can provide fresh insights.

## Our added value

Our added value lies in our feel for the care recipient, our ability to attune closely to their different levels, and exploring together. Being afraid of possible aggression is the biggest pitfall we've seen — we don't play that game: if you're afraid of your care recipient, how is he supposed to feel?
