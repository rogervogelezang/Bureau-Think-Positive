With active listening you try to understand the care recipient's feelings, so you can respond well to what they need. This calls for a degree of empathy and, above all, good listening. You then try to put the feelings you've picked up into words — to translate them. In doing so, you show acceptance of the care recipient's problems, you demonstrate understanding for their situation, and at the same time you encourage them to look for solutions.

What matters here is that you show genuine interest in what the care recipient thinks and feels — make sure they notice that you care.

**Purpose**

Active listening usually leads to a better relationship between carer and care recipient. It helps avoid misunderstandings, resolve conflicts and build trust.

Being heard and understood is so satisfying that you feel warmth towards the listener. The listener experiences something similar: when you really listen to someone, you come to understand them, and you soon feel involved and fond of them.

**Example**

"How did it go today?"

"Badly."

"Yes, you really look down. What happened?"

"Oh, we've got a new nurse. She goes far too fast."

"You're afraid you can't keep up?"

"Yes, I asked her to explain something again, and she said I should just pay better attention."

"Hm. And how did that make you feel?"

## No-lose method

Active listening, following on from Rogers, was also described by Gordon. He linked it to the 'no-lose method' for resolving conflicts between parents and children. Active listening mainly consists of asking further questions about how someone is experiencing things, and of giving 'I-messages'.

The aim is to actively avoid listening blocks and to get the conversation going and keep it going, for example by:

- inviting the other person to say more;
- trying to connect what is said with your own experiences;
- helping to pick up the thread again, for example by briefly summarising what has already been said;
- asking follow-up questions.

In non-verbal terms, active listening is possible through attentive closeness shown in body language, and through encouraging gestures and sounds. The no-lose method assumes that no single person calls the shots, has the final word or is proven right, but that everyone involved in the process has equal opportunities and a say. Handling feedback well — both giving it and receiving it — is therefore extremely important.

## Active listening, why?

When others get the impression that we understand them, that we listen to them and respect their point of view, they'll feel that they don't need to actively search for recognition. This creates an opening for guiding them. What's more, it has a de-escalating effect, which is a welcome bonus.
