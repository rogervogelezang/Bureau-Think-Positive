Sanctions (consequences) for the orthopedagogical setting, from a competence-focused approach.

## General

- A respectful approach.
- Take the child seriously.
- Listen to the child.
- However, sanctions are sometimes necessary to help the child stop negative behaviour and make clear that something isn't acceptable.
- Don't just tell the child what they're doing wrong, but especially what they're doing right.

## Sanctions

A number of things are important when using sanctions:

- It must be clear to everyone which rules and sanctions are used within the group.
- When you receive a sanction, we look at what works best for you in that particular situation — so preferably no standard punishments.
- When you receive a sanction, it's very important that it's clear which sanction you're getting, why you're getting it, and how long it will last.

When it comes to which sanction you receive, it's also important that:

- the sanction relates to what you've done. So if you've broken something belonging to someone else, you should, for example, buy a new one and offer your apologies;
- you're able to deal with the sanction as quickly as possible, so you don't have to carry it around with you for long and can move on;
- we look closely at whether you're learning from it;
- simply sitting in your room isn't good on its own. It's better, if you are sitting in your room, to use that time to think about what happened and how you could have handled it better.

### What officially aren't sanctions

- **Being sent to sit apart (stairs/chair)** — this is used to teach you to calm down for a moment and quickly give you the chance to show good behaviour and rejoin the group again. This doesn't work for everyone. It must be discussed properly with you beforehand, and it must also be checked whether this works for you.
- **Being placed in seclusion** — this can never be used as a sanction. It's only necessary in situations where you're a danger to yourself or those around you.

## Principles for imposing sanctions (Slot & Spanjaard, 2003)

- They don't infringe on the rights and integrity of young people.
- A sanction must be proportionate and not last too long.
- A sanction must be given consistently and follow directly on from the (undesired) behaviour.
- In terms of content, a sanction should correspond as closely as possible to the (undesired) behaviour it relates to — the sanction must be linked to the behaviour.
- Sanctions fit the young person's difficulties and developmental level.
- Sanctions are applied before there is any question of escalation.
- Sanctions are always accompanied by guidance on the appropriate behaviour.
- The young person must be able to make a connection between the undesired behaviour and the sanction. An explanation from the care worker is always needed. If the young person doesn't understand why they're receiving a sanction, this can provoke aggression or resistance, and the sanction loses its educational value. The sanction must therefore be clearly formulated for the young person and for the other parties involved — the young person must understand the reason for the sanction.
- Sanctions must be evaluated for their usefulness. When sanctions don't have the desired effect (stopping the undesired behaviour), it isn't useful to keep coming up with new sanctions. What matters then is working together to look for other, more creative solutions.
- When determining and administering sanctions, it's important not to let yourself be guided by your own emotions. Besides the fact that emotions get in the way of a clear view of your own actions, there's a risk that the young person will connect the sanction to "you as a person" rather than to the undesired behaviour.
- Giving a time-out is a common and useful pedagogical tool. Its purpose is to remove the young person from the situation that's reinforcing their inadequate behaviour. Teams regularly discuss the use of time-outs as a pedagogical tool during team meetings. Each team looks at which time-out options are available (a room, going outside, another space) or can be created, with a chair or stairs used as little as possible for the reasons mentioned above.

## Points of attention when imposing sanctions

- Sanctions shouldn't be fixed in advance. This can lead young people to calculate their behaviour, deciding to show undesired behaviour with this knowledge in mind. A standard sanction also limits the ability to tailor the sanction to the individual person and situation — whereas the approach starts from the uniqueness of every young person and their situation.
- Also pay attention to whether sanctions are actually enforceable. If they can't be carried out, this has the opposite effect.
