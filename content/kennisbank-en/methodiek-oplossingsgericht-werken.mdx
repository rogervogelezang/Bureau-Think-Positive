A method developed in America: Solution Focused Therapy (SFT). It emerged from the 1980s onwards (Berg, Shazer and others), largely as a reaction against existing psychotherapy, with a focus on 'the ways in which people find solutions'. Its roots lie in communication and systems theory and (cognitive) behavioural therapy (M.H. Erikson, J. Haley and others). Among other things, this means that problems are no longer viewed in terms of their limitations, but that the primary, concrete and practical focus is on how people usually solve problems and which possibilities and skills they already possess themselves.

There is a fundamental difference between thinking and acting aimed at solving problems, and thinking and acting aimed at working out solutions. People have their own resources. Where these are lacking, people may well have the ability to expand or strengthen them, or to approach the situation in a solution-focused way. In this way, all the attention lies with the client themselves. The practitioner's task is to give proper shape to this attention and help the client focus concretely on the solution.

## Solution-focused questions

This happens step by step, using an arsenal of solution-focused questions. These act as 'pats on the back and hints' — the practitioner is a 'guide with a focus on the future'. The type of question is determined by the various elements that play a role in the process. The conversation-oriented approach recognises six types of questions:

1. About change before the first conversation
2. About exceptions
3. About the goal
4. "And what else?"
5. Scaling questions
6. About competencies

A key question is the so-called **miracle question**: what would be different if you woke up tomorrow morning and all your problems were solved?

## Taking the other person into account

It helps to take into account the person you are asking the questions of. Roeden and others use a threefold division for this: 'visitor, complainant and customer'. Besides the verbally oriented approaches — which are also adapted for people with a mild intellectual disability — there are alternative forms of solution-focused working: stories, drama, role play, working with animals, drawings, music, video and more.
