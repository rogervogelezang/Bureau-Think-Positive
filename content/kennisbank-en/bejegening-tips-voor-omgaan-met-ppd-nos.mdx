## Practical approach

- Respond well to the fact that children with PDD-NOS cope poorly with changing situations. Take this into account, and don't hold it against them.
- Avoid abstract language as much as possible, as they won't understand it. For these children, language should above all be functional.
- Children with PDD-NOS hardly use looking and listening at all (they rely more on smell and touch). They need to consciously learn this skill. Give the child a reason to listen or look, for example through structured tasks.
- Children with PDD-NOS like to keep everything the same. So introduce changes very slowly and adjust plans step by step. Try to pre-empt panic situations as much as possible by preparing the child well.
- Also make clear exactly how a task will unfold (what, when, how, with whom, and so on) and how long it will take (schedule, reward for a good result). Provide visual support too, for example through a picture story or pictograms, so the child can see at a glance what the task involves.
- Use reward systems to help the child over the threshold. Good rewards include material rewards (getting something for doing well) — usually very powerful reinforcers for these children —, activity reinforcers (reading a comic book, stapling booklets together for the teacher), exchange reinforcers (stickers) and social reinforcers (getting a compliment).
- Only teach behaviour through plenty of repetition, programming it in, prompting and explaining.
- Always keep the child grounded with both feet on the ground. Flights of fantasy can easily turn into fear.
- Help him realise that he has limitations and a way of thinking that differs from others. This makes him more realistic and can help increase his self-acceptance.

## Tips for carers

- Make sure the child doesn't determine too much of what happens in the family. Keep hold of the reins yourself — the other children need attention and space too.
- Be careful about how you express your feelings. Emotions such as hostility, criticism or over-involvement are particularly bad for your child: they are often not understood and actually raise stress levels.
- Also make sure you take some distance from each other from time to time. Irritation and stress increase when you spend more than 35 hours a week together.
- Provide order, routine and structure.
- Never accept aggressive behaviour from your child.
- Try to introduce, change or practise new things step by step.
- Make new things predictable; use visual material if needed, such as pictograms or a picture story.
- Stop unwanted behaviour early. The longer you wait, the harder it becomes to unlearn it.
- Encourage desired behaviour, such as playing together with other children and visiting the doctor, so your child gets used to it.
- Persevere and don't be put off by resistance when new things need to be learned. Do make sure the situation stays safe for the child.
